About

How does the Sun's atmosphere become so hot, and what can its smallest structures tell us about the physics at work?

I study the solar chromosphere and corona using coordinated observations from IRIS, SDO, Hinode and the Swedish 1-m Solar Telescope. By bringing those measurements together with radiative magnetohydrodynamic simulations such as Bifrost, I investigate how magnetic fields shape the atmosphere and how energy moves through it.

Alongside solar physics, I work on machine learning for scientific imaging: instrument calibration, image translation, uncertainty-aware reconstruction and anomaly detection. I lead numerical validation work for NASA's upcoming MUSE mission, which launches in 2027.

I completed my PhD at the University of Oslo in 2021 with a thesis on spicules and mass flows, which the International Astronomical Union named the best PhD thesis of the year (globally) in solar and heliospheric physics. I then spent nearly four years as a Research Scientist at the Lockheed Martin Solar & Astrophysics Laboratory in Palo Alto before returning to Oslo in 2026 as a researcher (PI).

Research

From the fine structure of the solar atmosphere to the methods that help us see it

Heating the chromosphere and corona

How magnetic structure and electric currents translate into heating across atmospheric layers. Our Nature Astronomy study found that chromospheric heating and coronal emission in plage correlate on scales of a few hundred kilometres, matching a simulation in which braided current sheets dissipate.

Bose et al. 2024, Nature Astronomy

Spicules and mass flows

Following small, rapidly evolving jets to understand how mass and energy move between layers: their multi-thermal downflows, their formation in Ca II K and Mg II k, and whether they leave a signature at million-degree temperatures.

Bose et al. 2025, ApJ Letters

Small-scale magnetism and the corona

What the chromosphere underneath coronal bright points looks like, how flux emergence sets off chains of energetic events, and where energy release localises within magnetic flux tubes.

Bose et al. 2023, ApJ

Machine learning for solar observations

Image translation, autocalibration of SDO/AIA, uncertainty-aware reconstruction, diffusion-based super-resolution and normalising-flow anomaly detection, with an emphasis on knowing when a model's output can be trusted. This feeds directly into the MUSE mission pipelines.

Interpretability research

Understanding what language models learn, layer by layer

Alongside solar physics, I do research in AI safety and mechanistic interpretability. In 2026 I was selected for the Spring cohort of SPAR, the Supervised Program for Alignment Research, from around 4,500 applicants, working under the supervision of Yuxiao Li.

My SPAR project, SelectiveLoRA, asks where in a language model fine-tuning should happen. I compare LoRA adapters placed on all layers, on late layers only, and on randomly chosen layers, and measure both what the model gains on the target task and what it forgets elsewhere. The experiments show clear catastrophic forgetting under non-selective fine-tuning, and preliminary results indicate that instruction tuning changes which placement strategy works best.

I also build and analyse small transformer models to study attention circuits directly, using QK and OV matrices, head ablations and visualisation. The questions are close to the ones I ask of solar data: what a model has actually learned, and when its output can be trusted.

    Scientific machine learning

    Uncertainty-aware reconstruction

    A self-contained PyTorch workflow that reconstructs noisy, compressed or masked images with a convolutional autoencoder and returns per-pixel epistemic uncertainty via Monte Carlo dropout. Runs on CPU, MPS or CUDA.
